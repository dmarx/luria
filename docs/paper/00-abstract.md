# Abstract {.unnumbered}

A body of writing is usually described by what it contains. This paper
describes one by what it can do when something in it stops being true. I argue
that a corpus is *living* not because it grows, nor because it is edited, but
because it can change its mind accountably, and that this capacity has three
parts: the corpus distinguishes the *standing* of what it holds from the bare
fact that it holds it; it makes *dependence* among its parts explicit, so that
a change of standing in one place is felt in others; and it records the
*judgments* that interrupt such propagation, with their reasons. A
consequence is that staleness is a relational property, a "Cambridge change"
in Geach's sense: a sentence can become false overnight without a character
of it changing.

Following H. L. A. Hart's account of law as the union of primary and
secondary rules, I argue that a pile of notes stands to a living corpus as a
pre-legal society stands to a legal system, and that the architecture of a
living corpus is the architecture of secondary rules for knowledge: rules of
recognition, of change, and of adjudication. Luria, a command-line framework
that keeps such a corpus as plain files, is introduced as the motivating
example, and its record of itself (126 decisions and 18 principles at the time
of writing) serves as a case study. The argument is conceptual. The evidence is
one system used on itself, not a controlled evaluation, and I say where that
limits what can be claimed.

**Keywords:** knowledge management; truth maintenance; belief revision;
institutional facts; documentation; design rationale; Luria
