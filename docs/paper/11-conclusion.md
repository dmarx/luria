# Conclusion

A corpus does not become alive by accumulating or by being edited. It becomes
alive when it can answer three questions about itself: *what do we hold now*,
*what rests on what*, and *who decided that a doubtful thing should stand, and
why*. These are the questions that legal systems answered by adding secondary
rules to primary ones, and I have argued that a corpus is in the same position
and needs the same addition. The addition is architectural. It is a matter of
what distinctions the corpus draws (identity from standing, source from view,
finding from judgment, subject from governance) and not of how conscientious its
authors are.

The central observation is small. A sentence can become false without a
character of it changing, because staleness is a Cambridge change, and any
system that watches documents and not relations is blind to it. Everything
else follows from refusing to be blind: representing the relation, giving
standing a vocabulary, halting at the question and not answering it, and making
the answer a recorded act with a reason.

Luria is one implementation, and its own record is the one case study. The
case shows that the distinctions can be drawn in plain files and a lint, and
that a corpus can use them on itself. It does not show that they help anyone
else, that the cost of keeping them is lower than the cost of the staleness
they prevent, or that the particular choices (closed vocabularies, a
warn-by-default dial, acknowledgement by comment) are the best ones. Those are
questions for adopters and for measurement. What I would claim is narrower: that
the criterion, a corpus that can change its mind accountably, is the right one
to build toward, and that the failure it targets is real, common, and almost
invisible from inside the documents that suffer it.

Shereshevsky could not forget, and so could not think. The remedy for a corpus
in his condition is not a worse memory. It is a way of keeping what it knew
while saying, clearly and with reasons, what it now holds.
